Equation of State for Solid Phase I of Carbon Dioxide Valid for Temperatures
up to 800 K and Pressures up to 12 GPa
J. P . Martin Truslera)
Department of Chemical Engineering, Imperial College London, South Kensington Campus, London SW7 2AZ, United Kingdom
(Received 23 September 2011; accepted 8 November 2011; published online 30 December 2011)
The available thermodynamic-property data for solid phase I of carbon dioxide (“dry
ice”) are reviewed and used to determine the parameters of a new fundamental equation
of state constructed in the form of a Helmholtz energy function with temperature and
molar volume as the independent variables. The experimental data considered include the
pressure, molar volume, and isobaric heat capacity along the sublimation curve, the
melting-pressure curve, and molar volume in the compressed solid at temperatures
from 295 to 764 K and pressures up to 12 GPa. The equation of state is based on
the quasi-harmonic approximation, incorporating a Debye oscillator distribution for the
vibrons, two discrete modes for the librons and a further three distinct modes for the
internal vibrations of the CO2 molecule. A small anharmonic correction term is included,
which is signiﬁcant mainly in the region of the triple point. The estimated relative
uncertainty of molar volume at speciﬁed temperature and pressure calculated from the
equation of state is 0.02% on the sublimation curve and 1.5% in the compressed solid;
for isobaric heat capacity on the sublimation curve, the uncertainty varies from 5.0% to
0.5% between 2 and 195 K. Auxiliary equations for the pressure and molar volume on
the sublimation and melting curves are given. The equation of state is valid at tempera-
tures from 0 to 800 K and at pressures from the solid–ﬂuid phase boundary to 12
GPa. VC 2011 American Institute of Physics . [doi:10.1063/1.3664915]

1. Introduction
Carbon dioxide is a very common and well-studied sub-
stance. On earth, it is found in the atmosphere, oceans, upper
mantle, and crust, and has profound effects in each. CO 2 is
also found in the atmosphere or, as solid, on the surfaces
of some planets. Interests in the solid phases of CO 2 in
the ﬁelds of earth and planetary sciences divide between the
high-temperature high-pressure properties, relevant in the
upper mantle, 1 and low-temperature behavior, relevant to
surface deposits of CO2 on cold planets and their satellites.2,3
Carbon dioxide is also of major technological importance
and the demands of large-scale CO 2 capture, transport, and
storage call for an understanding of all the phases of this sub-
stance.4 In particular, the solid is likely to form in the event
of rupture of or leakage from high-pressure transmission
lines operating with CO 2, and knowledge of the thermody-
namic properties is required to model such events and to de-
velop safety cases. The normal solid (“dry ice”) is
scientiﬁcally interesting as the classical example of an orien-
tationally ordered molecular crystal, and there is also much
interest in the other phases of solid CO 2, which are observed
under extreme compression.
As a molecule, CO 2 is exceptionally stable and character-
ized by a rigid linear structure having a substantial electric
quadrupole moment. The intermolecular forces between
pairs of CO 2 molecules are dictated largely by short-range
repulsions, dispersion forces, and the quadrupole–quadrupole
interaction, all of which are anisotropic in nature. The quad-
rupole moment is thought to be responsible for the relatively
high triple-point and critical pressures of CO 2 (0.517 MPa
and 7.38 MPa, respectively), and the interplay of the differ-
ent intermolecular forces gives rise to polymorphism in the
solid phase. The thermodynamic properties of the ﬂuid
phases have been extensively studied and are represented at
temperatures up to 1100 K and pressures up to 800 MPa by a
reference-quality equation of state. 5 Many properties of dry
ice have been measured, but there exists no comprehensive
thermodynamic model that reconciles all of the available
data. This can be regarded as a gap in present scientiﬁc
knowledge of the nature of solid CO 2 I and also as a handi-
cap in engineering calculations related to processes in which
solid CO 2 is formed intentionally or accidentally. Thus the
main purpose of the present work was to provide a soundly
based thermodynamic model from which the properties of
solid CO 2 I may be obtained over the full temperature and
pressure ranges of its existence.

3. Previous Thermodynamic Models
No fundamental equation ﬁtted to a wide range of experi-
mental data has yet been published. A number of modeling
studies using intermolecular potentials of increasing complex-
ity have been reported10–16 involving consideration of thermo-
dynamic properties, lattice vibrations, and phase transitions of
solid CO2. It is quite possible that an intermolecular potential
model could be sufﬁciently reﬁned to describe all of these
properties to within experimental error. However, such an
approach would not be easy to apply, as stochastic techniques
are required to obtain the observable properties of the system.
Giordano et al. 17 derived a closed-form thermal equation of
state on the basis of experimental pressure–volume–temperature
data. Their equation provides an excellent description of the
high-pressure compression data at T /C21295 K and a good
description of the molar volume along the sublimation curve.
However, caloric properties were not considered and so, as it
stands, the equation of state cannot be used to calculate heat
capacity or other related properties. Span and Wagner5 devel-
oped a correlation for the sublimation pressure that was ﬁtted
to critically assessed data atT /C21154 K and is characterized by
uncertainties of /C20 250 Pa. A correlation of the melting curve
for T /C20 266 K was also presented. In 2006, Giordano et al. 8
reported a correlation of the melting curve, based on new
measurements, valid for temperatures up to 800 K.
The objective of the present work was to represent all of
the available experimental data, ideally to within the experi-
mental uncertainty, by means of a fundamental equation of
state from which all thermodynamic properties of solid phase
I could be computed. When coupled with an equation of state
for the ﬂuid phases, such a model would also permit calcula-
tion of the solid–ﬂuid phase boundaries.
4. Available Experimental Data
Many thermodynamic properties of solid phase I of carbon
dioxide have been studied experimentally to some extent.
However, the amount of available data is limited, and the
distribution of states investigated is not as wide as might be
desired. Accordingly, it is necessary to consider all the avail-
able data carefully and to include even some rather old work
in order to have sufﬁcient information to derive a wide-
ranging equation of state. Ideally, experiments would be per-
formed on single crystals, free of defects, but many of the
reported data relate to polycrystalline samples and this may
be responsible for some of the discrepancies that exist
between some data sources.
The collected data have been assigned to one of three
groups. Group 1 contains data tabulated in peer-reviewed
sources that either include an assessment of the experimental
uncertainties or provide sufﬁcient information to support rea-
sonable estimates thereof. Group 1 data were used in the opti-
mization of the parameters in the equation of state. Data in
group 2 were read from graphs; or lack a reliable estimate of
uncertainty; or pertain to the same property and range of states
as a group 1 dataset having lower uncertainty; or require for
their interpretation other thermodynamic properties that may
not be known with commensurate accuracy. Nevertheless,
data from group 2 are useful as a means of veriﬁcation. A few
of the datasets found were thought to be unreliable; these
were assigned to group 3 and not considered further.

5. Thermodynamic Modeling Basis
Given the sparse data set available, a theoretically based
equation of state was favored containing a relatively small
number of parameters, most of which have a physical inter-
pretation and hence some range of expected values. The
quasi-harmonic approximation has been widely used to
obtain the equation of state of inorganic crystalline solids
and has been applied previously to solid CO2.17 This approx-
imation was felt to be a useful basis for establishing the ther-
modynamic model in this work.

8. Uncertainty of the Equation of State
Based on the comparison with experimental data presented
in Figs. 4 and 5, the estimated relative uncertainty of the equa-
tion of state for molar volume is 0.02% on the sublimation
curve, increasing to 1.5% in the compressed solid. For iso-
baric heat capacity on the sublimation curve, the estimated
relative uncertainties are: 5% for T < 10 K; 3% for 10 /C20 T/K
/C20 35; and 0.5% for T > 35 K. It is not possible to provide a
substantiated uncertainty estimate for the heat capacity of the
compressed solid, as there are no experimental data with
which to compare. However, in the limits of high temperature
and high density, the lattice heat capacity is expected to
approach the Dulong–Petit limit of 5R and the quasi-harmonic
part of the equation of state conforms to this exactly. The
anharmonic correction term in the equation of state lowers the
lattice heat capacity only slightly, e.g., by about 0.5% at
T ¼ 750 K and p ¼ 10 GPa. It is also noticeable that the contri-
bution of the internal modes is very close to that found in the
gas phase. Thus there is a reasonable expectation that the pre-
dicted constant-volume heat capacity should behave reason-
ably in the compressed solid at high temperatures.

the same temperature. There is a local maximum in Cp,m at
the triple point caused by the large values of the thermal
expansivity in that most expanded state. A sharp peak is seen
in Fig. 18 at the triple point because of the discontinuity
there in the p-T locus along which Cp,m is plotted.
The three Gru¨neisen parameters associated with the exter-
nal lattice modes vary slowly with molar volume and span a
range of approximately 1 to 4, whereas spectroscopic studies
of these modes yield values close to 2. 17 The thermal
Gru¨neisen parameter, obtained from Eq. (39), is plotted in
Fig. 19. The isotherms for this property do not converge at
high temperatures because of the contribution of the internal
modes to CV,m. If the lattice heat capacity were used in place
of the thermodynamic CV,m, then the computed Gru ¨neisen
parameter would be approximately 2 at high temperatures.
The equation of state can also be used to explore metasta-
ble crystalline states. At p ¼ 0.1 MPa, the solid is thermody-
namically stable with respect to the vapor at temperatures up
to about 195 K. However, the condition for mechanical sta-
bility, namely ð@2Gm=@V2
mÞT < 0, is satisﬁed for the meta-
stable solid to considerably higher temperatures and,
according to the new equation of state, the absolute limit of
mechanical stability is not reached until T ¼ 235 K. As al-
ready mentioned, the equation of state may also be extrapo-
lated to very high pressures to obtain properties of solid
phase I in what are probably metastable states at p > 12 GPa.
The choice of the quasi-harmonic approximation leads to
an equation of state in the form of a Helmholtz function with
T and Vm as the independent variables. This approach was
favored because of its theoretical basis, even though a Gibbs
function such as that adopted for ice Ih by Feistel and
Wagner,75 with T and p as the independent variables, would
be somewhat more convenient in application. In the present
case, when T and p are speciﬁed, it is ﬁrst necessary to solve
the equation of state numerically for Vm(T,p), but this is very
easily achieved with a standard root ﬁnder (e.g., the New-
ton–Raphson method) and thereafter all properties follow
from simple analytical expressions.
The cold curve used in this work does not correspond
exactly to any of the simple intermolecular potential models
that might be applied to the system. However, it can be rep-
resented approximately by a central Mie potential, con-
strained to give the equilibrium molar volume at T ¼ 0K ,
having an attractive exponent of 6 and a repulsive exponent
of 10.87. However, this model does not match the accuracy
of the present equation of state. The Vinet equation can also
provide a reasonable representation of the cold curve.70
11. Conclusions
The present work provides for the ﬁrst time an equation of
state for solid phase I of carbon dioxide from which all ther-
modynamic properties may be obtained with some conﬁdence
at pressures up to approximately 12 GPa and temperatures up
to 800 K. The model is simple and easy to implement.
The equation of state is probably as good as it could be,
given the data available at the present time. New measure-
ments of the compression behavior at low temperatures
would be useful, as there are no reliable data at T < 295 K,
and for the compressibility along the sublimation curve.
The latter would be best addressed by means of sound-
speed measurements on single crystals. The equation of
